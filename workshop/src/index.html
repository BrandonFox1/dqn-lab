The DQN Workshop

A Deep Q-Network learns by grading its own guesses. After every move it asks one question: how far off was my guess, compared with what actually happened plus my guess about what happens next? Then it nudges its weights to shrink that gap. The four stations below let you poke at that loop until it clicks. On the first three you train small networks yourself; on the fourth, a much bigger one that learned offline plays the real arcade rules.

Things to try

  • Press Sweep a few times. Each sweep grades every square once, against a frozen snapshot of the previous sweep, and the color spreads exactly one square further from the cherry. That is the reward leaking backward.
  • Slide future discount down to 0.5. Far squares barely care about the cherry anymore: the glow fades fast with distance.
  • Switch to Network and press One step. A single update tints many squares at once, because they share weights. That sharing is why DQN uses a network: it can guess about squares it has never visited.
  • After it has learned, move the cherry. Watch the old knowledge get overwritten.

Arcade

Now the whole machine: a brand-new network plays this maze, stores every moment in a replay memory, and learns from random handfuls of it using exactly the update from the Bench. It starts out walking into walls and ghosts. Keep an eye on the exam line under the stats: expect it to start climbing after roughly 20,000 steps, a few minutes on Turbo. Four ghosts

The whole 1980 arcade game: the real maze, four ghosts with their own personalities, scatter and chase waves, frightened mode, Cruise Elroy, fruit, lives, and levels that speed up. It is rebuilt rule by rule from the documents that describe the original (listed at the bottom of this station) and drawn in this workshop’s style. A DQN learned to play it with the same update as the Bench, only bigger: a network of about half a million weights, trained for hours.

How the brain learned

The line is the exam: every 250,000 decisions the brain played the same 10 games with no learning and no deliberate random moves, on the same sticky stick it trained with. Dots are practice games: the average of the last 100 finished while learning, random exploration moves included. Dashed line: random play.

Practice runs far below the exam, and not because the brain plays worse there. Each of the 16 training games makes a different share of its moves at random, from 0.07% to 40%, and the most random games end soonest, so they fill almost half of the finished games. Replaying the brain on this page that way, the 5 games with 5% or more random moves averaged 9,304 points; the other 11 averaged 26,483.

The rules, and where each one comes from

Built from scratch for this page: an 11,908-weight network with hand-written backpropagation, Adam, replay memory, a frozen target copy and Double DQN, all running in your browser. Same algorithm as the pacdqn project, smaller maze.

The Four ghosts brain was trained offline in PyTorch with the same ingredients (replay memory, a frozen copy, Double DQN) on the same arcade engine this page runs, and plays here through the page’s own network code. The rules come from public documents; the characters and artwork are original.
